A pie is a 100% stacked bar bent into a circle.
That is the whole idea, and it is worth starting from, because it means rhp does most of the work.

**The spans.**
`shares` turns the raw numbers into percentages and `stackUp` turns those into a `from` and a `to` for each part:

```js
const stack = createMemo(() => stackUp(shares(hours())));
```

So part one runs 0 to 37, part two 37 to 61, and so on to 100.

**One box for all of them.**
The `Plot` has `overlap`, which puts every slat in the same box rather than in a band of its own.
Flat that is a stacked bar, one segment after another. Round it is a pie.
Each slat holds a `Bar` from its `from` to its `to`, and rhp writes those two ends as `--rhp-lo` and `--rhp-hi`, both 0 to 1.
A `Label` at the middle of the span gets `--rhp-p`, which is where its wedge points.

**The one thing rhp cannot draw** is the outline of a wedge.
An outline with rounded corners is a path, which is why rhp's own `Area` and `Line` are paths too.
The `Bar` keeps the numbers and gives up its box, and an `<svg>` inside it draws the wedge in a 100 by 100 box around the middle.

**The gap has to be a distance, not an angle.**
This is the part that is easy to get wrong.
Pad the angles instead, and the gap is wide at the rim and closes to nothing at the middle, where every wedge meets.
So each straight edge is not rotated, it is pushed sideways by a fixed amount:

```js
const cross = (t, r, off) => t + Math.asin(Math.max(-1, Math.min(1, off / r))) / TAU;
```

That gives the turn at which a pushed edge crosses the circle of radius `r`.
The edge then runs parallel to the true one, so the paper between two wedges is one width the whole way out.

**The corners are rounded by a stroke**, not by the path.
`fill` and `stroke` in the same color make one solid shape, and `stroke-linejoin: round` rounds every corner of it:

```css
.slice path { fill: var(--rhp-color); stroke: var(--rhp-color); stroke-width: 3.8; stroke-linejoin: round; }
```

The stroke also grows the wedge by half its width in every direction.
So the path is drawn that much smaller at the rim, and the edges are pushed that much further apart to pay for what the stroke eats back.

Two smaller things follow from drawing the shape yourself:

- Each arc is always the same number of cubics rather than an `A` command, so one wedge's path has the same shape as the next and `transition: d` can move between them.
- The two versions end a wedge differently, and it is worth comparing them.
  The simple version is a plain pie: its two pushed edges are carried until they cross, that crossing is the tip, and no corner is rounded.
  The gallery version starts each wedge at a small inner radius instead, so all four ends of every wedge sit on one circle and one stroke can round all four corners at once.
  The inner radius is what makes that possible: with a tip there is nothing for a stroke to round without pulling the tip back off the middle.

**Flat, none of this runs.**
The `<svg>` is hidden and rhp draws the bar itself, with `--rhp-gap` for the space between segments and `--rhp-radius` for their corners.
The same two numbers, spent on a length instead of an angle.

The gallery version adds:

- **A wedge that slides out.**
  CSS works the direction out itself, from the numbers rhp already wrote: `sin()` and `cos()` of the angle halfway along the span.
- **Only the wedge catches the pointer.**
  Under `overlap` every slat fills the plot, slat box and `<svg>` box alike, so whichever is last would catch the pointer everywhere.
  The slat is given `pointer-events: none` and the painted path and its label are given it back.
- **Tab and the arrow keys.**
  The `Plot` has `keyboard`, and the label's second line swaps from the name to the hours on hover or focus.
